Neural subgrid fluxes with convex limiting for the shallow-water equations

What we built, the maths behind it, and how training and inference work. Reimplementation of Timofeyev, Schwarzmann & Kuzmin, Math. Comput. Simul. 238 (2025) 163–178.

1. The idea

The shallow-water equations describe a water surface (a river, a coastal sea) by two fields: the depth \(h(x,t)\) and the discharge \(q=hv\) (depth × velocity). Resolving every wave takes a fine mesh: here 2000 cells and a time step of 0.005. A coarse mesh of 100 cells is 20× smaller and tolerates a much larger time step, but the standard scheme (local Lax–Friedrichs, LLF) is numerically diffusive and smears the waves away.

The paper's remedy: keep the cheap coarse solver and add a correction flux \(\tilde G\) predicted by a small neural network that was trained on fine-mesh data. A network can output anything, including a correction that drives the depth negative, so a limiter (MCL, monolithic convex limiting) clips \(\tilde G\) to a range in which the update is provably safe.

One line: coarse state \(U\) → coarse LLF flux \(\bar F\) + network correction \(\tilde G\) → MCL clips it to \(\tilde G^{*}\) → flux \(F=\bar F+\tilde G^{*}\) → time step → next coarse state.
DNS — direct numerical simulation: the fine 2000-cell run, treated as the truth.
Flux — how much \(h\) or \(q\) crosses a cell interface per unit time.
LLF — local Lax–Friedrichs: a robust but diffusive way to compute the flux between two cells.
Stencil — the neighbouring cells a formula looks at (here 4 cells: \(i-1,\dots,i+2\)).
Bar state — the averaged solution of the two-cell problem; the building block of MCL.
CFL condition — the time step must be small enough that no wave crosses a whole cell per step.
MCL — monolithic convex limiting: clips a correction so bounds such as \(h>0\) hold.
Heun — a second-order, two-stage time-stepping method.
Standardise — shift and scale data to zero mean, unit variance before the network sees it.
Early stopping — stop training when the validation loss stops improving (guards against over-fitting).

2. The maths, step by step

2.1 Equations

With \(u=[h,q]^T\) on a periodic domain \((0,L)\), \(L=100\), \(g=9.812\):

$$u_t+f(u)_x=0,\qquad f(u)=\Big[\,q,\ \tfrac{q^2}{h}+\tfrac12 g h^2\Big]^T .$$

2.2 The fine scheme (the "truth", DNS)

Finite volumes on \(N_f=2000\) cells (\(\Delta x=0.05\)). The LLF flux through interface \(i+\tfrac12\), with the largest local wave speed \(\lambda\):

$$F_{i+1/2}=\tfrac12\big(f(u_i)+f(u_{i+1})\big)-\tfrac12\lambda_{i+1/2}(u_{i+1}-u_i),\quad \lambda_{i+1/2}=\max\!\big(|v_i|+\sqrt{gh_i},\ |v_{i+1}|+\sqrt{gh_{i+1}}\big),\quad \tfrac{d}{dt}u_i=\tfrac{F_{i-1/2}-F_{i+1/2}}{\Delta x}.$$

Time stepping is Heun's method (two Euler stages averaged). The bar state \(\bar u_{i+1/2}=\tfrac12(u_i+u_{i+1})-\tfrac{f(u_{i+1})-f(u_i)}{2\lambda_{i+1/2}}\) rewrites each Euler stage as a convex combination of \(u_i\) and bar states whenever \(\tfrac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}(\lambda_{i-1/2}+\lambda_{i+1/2})\le1\) (the CFL condition), which is why the depth stays positive.

2.3 Coarse variables and the exact subgrid flux

Group \(k=20\) fine cells into a macrocell and average: \(U_i=\tfrac1k\sum_{j\in\text{cell }i}u_j\) (\(N_c=100\), \(\Delta X=1\)). Averaging the fine equation gives an exact coarse equation:

$$\tfrac{d}{dt}U_i=\frac{(\bar F_{i-1/2}+G_{i-1/2})-(\bar F_{i+1/2}+G_{i+1/2})}{\Delta X},\qquad G_{i+1/2}=F(u_{ki},u_{ki+1})-\bar F(U_i,U_{i+1}),$$

where \(\bar F\) is the same LLF formula applied to the coarse averages. \(G\) is "anti-diffusive": coarse LLF smooths, \(G\) sharpens it back (we checked this identity to \(10^{-16}\)). The catch: \(G\) needs the fine states, which a coarse run does not have.

2.4 Learning \(G\)

Approximate \(\tilde G_{i+1/2}=\mathcal N(U_{i-1},U_i,U_{i+1},U_{i+2};\omega)\): four neighbouring cells × \((h,q)\) = 8 inputs, 2 outputs \((G^h,G^q)\), three hidden layers of 128 neurons with leaky-ReLU (slope 0.01), 34,434 parameters, trained on the mean-squared error

$$\mathcal L(\omega)=\frac1M\sum_{j=1}^{M}\big\|\mathcal N(U^{(j)}_{i-1},\dots,U^{(j)}_{i+2};\omega)-G^{(j)}_{i+1/2}\big\|_2^2 .$$

2.5 Limiting with MCL

Write the corrected coarse update with limited bar states \(\bar U^{*,\pm}_{i+1/2}=\bar U_{i+1/2}\pm\tilde G^{*}_{i+1/2}/\Lambda_{i+1/2}\):

$$\tfrac{d}{dt}U_i=\tfrac1{\Delta X}\Big[\Lambda_{i-1/2}\big(\bar U^{*,+}_{i-1/2}-U_i\big)+\Lambda_{i+1/2}\big(\bar U^{*,-}_{i+1/2}-U_i\big)\Big].$$

MCL demands a local maximum principle: the new bar states must stay within the range of their neighbours, \(h_i^{\min}\le\bar h^{*,-}_{i+1/2}\le h_i^{\max}\) and \(h_{i+1}^{\min}\le\bar h^{*,+}_{i+1/2}\le h_{i+1}^{\max}\), with \(h_i^{\min/\max}=\min/\max(\bar h_{i-1/2},\bar h_{i+1/2})\), and the same for the velocity \(\bar v=\bar q/\bar h\). It is applied in two steps. Height first (eq. 27 of the paper):

$$\tilde G^{h,*}=\begin{cases}\min\!\big(\tilde G^h,\ \Lambda\min(\bar h-h_i^{\min},\ h_{i+1}^{\max}-\bar h)\big),&\tilde G^h\ge0\\[2pt] \max\!\big(\tilde G^h,\ \Lambda\max(\bar h-h_i^{\max},\ h_{i+1}^{\min}-\bar h)\big),&\tilde G^h<0\end{cases}$$

Then discharge: limit \(\Delta\tilde G^q=\tilde G^q-\tilde G^{h,*}\bar v\) to the analogous velocity interval (eq. 31) and set \(\tilde G^{q,*}=\tilde G^{h,*}\bar v+\Delta\tilde G^{q,*}\). Under the CFL condition the updated \(U_i\) is then a convex combination of admissible states, so \(h>0\) whatever the network outputs. If the network is accurate the limits are inactive and MCL changes nothing.

3. How it runs

Training

  1. Initial conditions. 200 random sine waves: \(h=2+A_h\sin(2\pi k_hx/L+\varphi_h)\), \(v=V_0+A_v\sin(2\pi k_vx/L+\varphi_v)\), with \(V_0\sim U(1,2)\), \(A\sim U(0.2,0.6)\), \(k\in\{1..6\}\), \(\varphi\sim U(0,2\pi)\).
  2. Labels. Run the fine DNS to \(T=80\). Every 10th step, average to 100 cells and compute the exact \(G\) at all 100 interfaces: 32 million (stencil, \(G\)) pairs. (The paper keeps every step, 320 M.)
  3. Standardise inputs and outputs (zero mean, unit variance); the last 20 % of trajectories are held out for validation.
  4. Optimise. SGD, batch 128. Stage 1: learning rate 0.05, up to 500 epochs; stage 2: 0.001, up to 2000; early stopping in both. Ours stopped after 32 and 18 epochs, validation loss \(3.96\cdot10^{-4}\) (standardised units).

Inference (each time step)

  1. Start. Box-average the fine initial condition to the 100-cell state \(U\); the fine solver is never used again.
  2. LLF flux \(\bar F\) at the 100 interfaces from \(U\).
  3. Network. Build each 4-cell stencil, standardise, evaluate \(\mathcal N\), undo the standardisation: raw \(\tilde G\).
  4. MCL (optional switch): \(\tilde G\to\tilde G^{*}\) by the two limiters of §2.5.
  5. Update. \(F=\bar F+\tilde G^{*}\), \(\dot U_i=(F_{i-1/2}-F_{i+1/2})/\Delta X\). Heun's method repeats steps 2–5 for its two stages. The time step is the paper's 0.005, or up to 0.1.

  • It works as the paper claims. On the paper's own cases the 100-cell net + MCL is 0.3–0.5 % off in \(q\) against 1.5–7 % for plain coarse LLF, and it keeps working at mean depths \(\pm25\) % from the training value and for runs 5× longer than the training trajectories (\(t=400\) against 80, \(q\) error 0.4 %).
  • MCL is a safety net, not an accuracy booster. Without it the net alone blows up in 5 of 20 cases (very shallow water, near-dry troughs, strong dam breaks); with it, in none. Errors there stay large (dam breaks 5–26 %) because the network never saw shocks.
  • MCL can also hurt. For waves resolved by only 5–7 coarse cells, clipping flattens real crests: \(q\) error 15 % with MCL against 2 % without.
  • Our addition (not in the paper). A mirror-symmetric network, with a built-in \(x\to-x\) symmetry that the exact \(G\) obeys: it fixes left-moving flow (4.3 % → 0.7 %) and cuts dam-break errors by 40–80 %.
  • From the paper: smaller networks. The paper also trains 3×64, 3×32 and 3×16 networks and finds 32 neurons often as good as 128. We trained only 3×32 (on half the data) and agree: with MCL its median error is 2.5 % against 2.9 % for the 3×128 network, with 14× fewer parameters.
  • The speed-up is modest. One network call costs about 90 µs in PyTorch, more than a whole 2000-cell DNS right-hand side in NumPy (about 60 µs). At the paper's \(\Delta t=0.005\) the reduced models are therefore slower than the DNS here; with \(\Delta t=0.05\) they are about 2–5× faster (plain coarse LLF: 17×).

Differences from the paper. Training uses every 10th time step (memory). Our Fig. 6 Lax–Wendroff baseline is less diffusive than the paper's (their variant is not specified). The network-size study (their Figs. 9–10) is only partly reproduced: we trained 3×128 and 3×32 but not 3×64 or 3×16. An earlier version of our code built the training target \(G\) from the wrong fine cell; this report uses the corrected code (a test checks the exact-\(G\) identity of §2.3).
